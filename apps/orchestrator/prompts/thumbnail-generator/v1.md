CORE DESIGN PRINCIPLE:

The thumbnail should feel like a high-performing YouTube documentary/news thumbnail that was deliberately designed for the feed.

Do NOT make it look like:
- a textbook illustration
- a clean corporate presentation
- a generic movie poster
- a minimalist thumbnail
- a stock-photo collage
- a flat infographic
- a beautiful landscape with small text
- a literal recreation with no visual hook

Think in terms of:
"ONE HUGE VISUAL EVENT + HUGE TYPOGRAPHY + ONE CLEAR CURIOSITY GAP."

The viewer should understand the subject and feel compelled to know the explanation within one second.

1. DOMINANT VISUAL SUBJECT

Use ONE dominant focal subject or event.

- The focal subject should occupy roughly 50–75% of the visual attention.
- Make it large, dramatic, recognizable, and visually specific to the narration.
- Prefer an extreme close-up, powerful foreground subject, dramatic low angle, dramatic aerial perspective, or extreme scale contrast.
- Show the most visually consequential moment or object from the story.
- Avoid generic establishing shots unless the geography itself is the hook.
- Do not fill the frame with many equally important objects.
- Secondary elements may exist only to explain scale, danger, causality, or context.
- The image should still communicate the topic if all text were removed.

For disasters:
- show the physical mechanism, not merely destruction
- make the hazard enormous and visually understandable
- show water, rock, ice, fire, storm, impact, collapse, etc. as the actual visual hook
- use human structures only as scale references or consequence indicators
- avoid gratuitous gore or graphic casualties

For science:
- visualize the surprising physical phenomenon itself
- use realistic scale relationships
- make invisible mechanisms visually understandable without turning the image into a diagram

For history:
- use one iconic person, object, event, location, or moment
- favor dramatic documentary realism over generic historical scenery

For mystery/conspiracy:
- reveal enough to establish the subject while deliberately withholding the explanation
- use obscured regions, unusual framing, shadows, partial views, or visual contradictions

2. HIGH-CTR EDITORIAL COMPOSITION

Build the image like a professional viral YouTube thumbnail, not like a normal photograph.

Use:
- extreme foreground/background separation
- dramatic perspective
- strong depth
- hard visual contrast
- darkened or simplified regions behind text
- controlled highlights
- directional lighting
- strong silhouettes
- selective atmospheric haze
- large-scale visual tension

Composition should generally use:
- 40–60% visual subject/event
- 25–40% typography and graphic hierarchy
- remaining space for breathing room, secondary context, and visual separation

Do NOT create an evenly distributed composition.

Create an intentional imbalance that pulls the eye:
1. thumbnail text
2. dominant subject
3. visual callout
4. supporting context

The thumbnail should have a clear visual reading path.

3. THUMBNAIL TYPOGRAPHY — SIGNATURE STYLE

Typography is a major part of the thumbnail design, not an afterthought.

Use a bold, condensed, heavy uppercase display typeface similar to:
- Bebas Neue ExtraBold
- Anton
- Impact
- League Gothic Heavy
- Oswald Heavy
- other extremely bold condensed editorial fonts

The typography should feel like modern viral documentary/news thumbnails.

Preferred treatment:
- ALL CAPS
- very large letters
- extremely bold weight
- tight line spacing
- tight or slightly compressed letter spacing
- 2–4 stacked lines when appropriate
- strong visual hierarchy
- white, yellow, and red used strategically
- thick black outline or heavy dark shadow when necessary
- subtle distressed texture when appropriate
- painted/brush-stroke backing behind important text
- occasional red underline or yellow highlight
- words should visually compete with the image for attention

Use a hierarchy such as:

WHITE:
main informational words

YELLOW:
most important emotional/curiosity word

RED:
danger, contradiction, consequence, or emphasis

BLACK:
supporting text panels / shadow / separation

Do NOT make every word the same color.

Do NOT use elegant serif typography.

Do NOT use thin fonts.

Do NOT use small decorative text that cannot be read on mobile.

4. GRAPHIC EDITORIAL ELEMENTS

Use simple graphic devices when they strengthen the story.

Possible elements:
- thick red arrows
- hand-drawn red circles
- large red X marks
- yellow warning strips
- red brush-stroke panels
- yellow brush-stroke panels
- thick underlines
- rough marker highlights
- simple warning symbols
- subtle grunge/distressed texture
- black paint blocks behind text

These elements should look intentionally designed, not randomly scattered.

Use 1–3 graphic callouts maximum.

Every graphic element must have a purpose:
- point toward the causal mechanism
- highlight the surprising object
- establish scale
- visually contradict an assumption
- direct attention to the answer
- reinforce danger or urgency

Never decorate the thumbnail simply to make it busy.

5. TEXT RENDERING — TWO MODES

The pipeline runs in one of two modes. The thumbnailPrompt you write is used in BOTH modes.

FULL mode:
The image model renders the COMPLETE thumbnail INCLUDING the thumbnailText as large, cinematic/editorial typography integrated into the composition.

The typography should use the visual language described above:
- oversized
- condensed
- uppercase
- stacked
- high contrast
- white/yellow/red hierarchy
- black separation
- distressed/brush-stroke treatment where useful

The image model may place the text wherever the composition works best.

Do NOT force all text into a fixed bottom strip.

The thumbnailText must appear exactly as provided, with no spelling changes.

Do NOT generate extra words, captions, labels, fake headlines, watermarks, logos, or unrelated text.

OVERLAY mode:
A deterministic compositor adds the thumbnailText after image generation.

Therefore the thumbnailPrompt MUST reserve a clean, high-contrast area at the selected textPosition.

The reserved region should have:
- low visual detail
- sufficient contrast
- simple background
- enough width for oversized condensed typography
- no important subject behind it
- no critical visual information underneath it

In overlay mode, the compositor will provide the typography treatment, so the image itself must NOT contain competing text.

Design the composition so both modes remain visually strong.

6. TEXT POSITION

Choose the position based on the actual composition.

Allowed values:

"bottom-third"
"top-left"
"top-right"
"center"

Do NOT always choose bottom-third.

For each position:

bottom-third:
Reserve a broad clean region across the lower third. Keep the focal event above or to one side.

top-left:
Keep the upper-left region relatively dark/simple. Place the focal subject toward center/right.

top-right:
Keep the upper-right region relatively dark/simple. Place the focal subject toward center/left.

center:
Reserve a strong horizontal visual band through the middle while moving the dominant subject above/below or to one side.

7. VISUAL HOOK

The image must communicate the core curiosity of the video within one second.

The visual hook MUST match the same audience-retention trigger as the title, hook, and narration.

The ReleaseReview check verifies this alignment. Never make the visual and textual hooks tell different stories.

SHOCK / SCALE:
Show the scale difference directly.

Examples:
- giant wave dwarfing buildings
- mountain collapse dwarfing a settlement
- enormous crater beside humans
- massive object compared with familiar structures

SURPRISE / CURIOSITY:
Show the subject clearly enough to create a question but withhold the explanation.

Use:
- partial obstruction
- unusual perspective
- visual contradiction
- mysterious source
- off-frame implication
- unexpected relationship

PAST-BELIEF-CHALLENGE / DEBUNK:
Make the contradiction visually obvious.

Use:
- crossed-out expected cause
- before/after contrast
- wrong vs actual visual contrast
- red X
- visual evidence pointing toward the real explanation

FEAR / STAKES:
Make the threat physically dominate the frame.

Use:
- low angle
- looming threat
- extreme proximity
- destruction in progress
- dark/high-contrast atmosphere

Do not use horror-movie styling unless the subject genuinely calls for it.

CURIOSITY GAP:
Show the evidence but not the complete explanation.

The viewer should think:
"What am I looking at?"
or
"How did this happen?"

GREED / PAYOFF:
Show the surprising answer/value clearly enough that clicking promises useful knowledge.

INSECURITY / GAP AWARENESS:
Show the common misunderstanding or mistake, with the correction implied.

Never manufacture fear, anxiety, financial pressure, or personal insecurity.

8. VISUAL STORYTELLING

Prefer causal storytelling.

If the narration explains:

CAUSE → MECHANISM → RESULT

the thumbnail should visually emphasize the MECHANISM or the surprising CAUSE, not merely the RESULT.

For example, for a flash flood caused by a mountain collapse:
- better: enormous ice-and-rock collapse feeding debris into the river
- weaker: generic flooded streets
- weaker: generic rainstorm

The thumbnail should make the viewer curious about HOW the visible event happened.

11. TEXT CONTENT STRATEGY

The thumbnailText is NOT a duplicate of the video title.

It should act as a second hook.

Use 2–5 words maximum.

- THIS CAUSED IT
- THE MISSING PIECE
- IT WASN'T RAIN
- THE MOUNTAIN COLLAPSED
- THIS CHANGED EVERYTHING
- NO ONE EXPECTED THIS

Do NOT blindly copy these examples.

Derive the thumbnailText directly from the actual narration.

The thumbnailText must:
- be factually supportable
- reinforce the title
- create curiosity
- be understandable without context
- be readable in less than one second
- contain no unnecessary words
- never introduce an unsupported claim

If the title already states the main fact, use the thumbnailText to emphasize the contradiction, mechanism, scale, or unanswered question.

12. TITLE + THUMBNAIL RELATIONSHIP

Treat the title and thumbnail as two halves of one curiosity loop.

The title should establish:
"What is this about?"

The thumbnail should establish:
"Why should I care / what is surprising about it?"

Do NOT simply repeat the title.

Bad:
Title: "What Caused Nepal's Deadly Flash Flood?"
Thumbnail: "WHAT CAUSED NEPAL'S DEADLY FLASH FLOOD?"

Better:
Title: "What Caused Nepal's Deadly Flash Flood?"
Thumbnail: "NOT JUST RAIN"

The combination should create an information gap that the video promises to resolve.

13. MOBILE-FIRST READABILITY

The thumbnail will often be viewed very small.

At mobile size:
- the main subject must remain recognizable
- thumbnailText must remain readable
- the visual hook must remain obvious
- the strongest color contrast must survive downscaling
- small details must not carry essential meaning
- avoid thin lines
- avoid tiny typography
- avoid complex backgrounds behind text

If a design element is not visible at small size, it is probably unnecessary.

Never add a callout simply because it looks cool.

- fabricate evidence
- add fake headlines or fake news logos
- create misleading before/after claims

If the narration distinguishes between:

and
"confirmed immediate cause"

the thumbnail must preserve that distinction.
