## Audience trigger delivery (mandatory)

The script activates an audience-retention trigger in three places: the hook (first 1–2 sentences), at least one escalation beat, and the ending. The editorial-guidelines "Audience Triggers" section is the canonical definition. The QA prompt verifies all three; you will be re-issued if any is missing.

**Hook delivery — the first sentence must land the trigger.** The trigger the ScriptPlanner selected is documented in the first beat's `keyMessage`. The hook's first sentence must make that trigger land for the viewer in plain language. Examples per trigger:

- Curiosity: "There is a place on Earth where your compass can point the wrong way."
- Surprise: "NASA launched a message into space that could outlive humanity."
- Shock (scale): "A 25-foot wave crashed through Boston. The terrifying part? It wasn't water."
- Past-belief-challenge: "Everyone says the Great Wall is the only thing visible from space. It isn't."
- Fear / stakes: "If this fault slips, millions have ninety seconds to react."
- Greed (pay-off preview): "Here is the one thing almost every solar-system diagram gets wrong."
- Insecurity (gap awareness): "Most people assume X. The research shows the opposite."

If the first sentence only sets the scene (a bare date or place, "Scientists discovered...", generic context) without the surprising outcome, the trigger has not landed and ScriptQA will reject the draft. Setting goes in the context beat unless it is part of the sentence that carries the outcome.

**Escalation beats must raise stakes or invert an assumption.** An escalation beat is not a restatement of the prior beat. Each escalation beat must do at least one of: introduce a number that reframes the prior claim, contrast the expected against the actual, name a trade-off or paradox, or invert what the audience just learned. A plain exposition beat is not an escalation.

**The ending must deliver the planned Ending Type (`{{endingType}}`) with a memorable payoff.** The final sentence is never "thanks for watching" or a generic summary. Implement the type exactly: resolved endings (`revelation`, `twist`, `callback`) close the central question with a payoff and do not need an extra open question; open endings (`unresolved_mystery`, `open_question`, `surprising_implication`) may leave one honest unresolved implication — only when the research is genuinely unresolved. When the research has a `debated` or `unverified_claim` fact, ending on that fact's honest uncertainty is a satisfying pay-off, not a failure.

**The ending must pay off the hook.** The hook is a promise; the ending is where it visibly completes. Write the ending so its first sentence reuses at least two distinctive content words from the hook (the hook's subject and its key noun or number) in plain language — question → answer, belief → correction, threat → resolution/outcome. If the hook names "a 25-foot wave", the ending should be able to say why that wave still matters, not merely close the topic. ScriptQA checks this deterministically (shared content words between the hook and the ending's opening) and reviews it for quality.

**Trigger honesty.** Every trigger must come from a research fact in the input. If you find yourself reaching for "nobody knows why", "scientists are baffled", or "this changes everything" as scaffolding, the trigger is not in the research — pick a documentary-explanation arc with strong curiosity hooks instead. ScriptQA flags any trigger that cannot be traced to a research fact under both factuality and trigger checks.

- The narration must be speakable in {{targetDurationSeconds}} seconds. Target a speaking rate of {{speakingRateWordsPerSecond}} words per second (e.g. ~{{targetWordRange}} words total). Match the target speaking time, not a fixed word count.

## Retention architecture (mandatory)

Treat the narration as a sequence of retention beats rather than one flat block. Viewer drop-off varies by format, topic, and audience; the figures below are production heuristics, not universal constants.

**Mid-script pivot — deliver the strongest available research-grounded escalation near the retention cliff.**

For short videos (≤90s), target the pivot at approximately 45% of runtime, but never later than the 30-second mark. For long videos (>90s), target 30–40% of total runtime. The 30-second bound for short-form is a hard ceiling, not a typical drop-off point.

Compute the target pivot position in narration words:

- Short-form: `pivotWords = round(min(30, targetDurationSeconds * 0.45) * speakingRateWordsPerSecond)`
- Long-form: `pivotWords = round(targetDurationSeconds * 0.35 * speakingRateWordsPerSecond)`

How to place it: write Beat 1 through the beat before Beat {{pivotBeatId}}, then count the words so far. Aim to have the pivot sentence start close to pivotWords, but it MUST sit entirely inside Beat {{pivotBeatId}}'s narration. If timing and beat placement conflict, the beat placement wins. Write the pivot as one sentence of at most 20 words, and do not make it the first or last sentence of the beat if you can avoid it (it should arrive after a short setup).

The pivot must be a single sentence that does ONE of:

- introduces a scale or number that reframes the prior claim;
- contrasts the expected against the actual;
- surfaces a paradox or counter-intuitive implication;
- signals a research-grounded payoff or escalation.

The pivot must be traceable to a research fact. Never invent a surprise, controversy, mystery, or implication to manufacture a pivot. When the plan names a Pivot Fact ID in the Story Beats, build the pivot from THAT fact.

The pivot must be distinct from the opening hook and ending payoff:

1. opening hook — creates curiosity;
2. mid-script pivot — escalates or reframes;
3. ending payoff — completes the planned ending type: a resolved ending closes the central question; an open ending may leave one unresolved implication or deeper question.

If the research contains no qualifying surprise, scale contrast, paradox, or counter-intuitive implication, use the strongest research-grounded escalation available at the pivot position. Do not force a fabricated twist. Do not artificially re-rank facts by shock value over narrative usefulness.

**Forward-pointing devices between the hook and the pivot (mandatory).**

The pivot targets ~45%/≤30s, but the beats BEFORE it are the scroll-away zone and need micro-hooks of their own. Every beat between the hook and the pivot must contain at least one forward-pointing device:

- stakes escalation — the next fact is strictly larger, more severe, or more consequential than this one (verified against research; never manufactured suspense);
- partial reveal — "the explanation involves X — but X alone does not account for Y";
- mini-refutation — the previous beat's implied conclusion does not hold.

Pure exposition is prohibited in every pre-pivot beat, not only at escalation beats. The device must ride on a research fact — if the research cannot support escalation, use a partial reveal on a real fact rather than padding with filler phrasing.

**Beat-boundary bridges (mandatory).**

Choppiness is created at the joins. Each beat's FIRST sentence must connect explicitly to the previous beat's LAST sentence, using one of:

- shared referent — "that ship", "this gap";
- temporal bridge — "seconds later", "for the next three years";
- causal bridge — "which is why", "because of this";
- contrast bridge — "but there was a problem".

Beat 1 is exempt (it is the hook). A join that could be reordered without changing the meaning is a restart, not a bridge. ScriptQA evaluates every join pair deterministically derived from your beat narrations.

**Open-loop payoffs (mandatory).**

The plan may declare an open-loop ledger per beat (`opensLoop`, `closesLoopFor`). Every beat that declares `closesLoopFor` must make its closure audible: its first sentence answers the earlier question in plain words and uses callback language — "Remember…", "That [thing] from earlier…", "Here's why…" — tying back to the opening phrasing. Combine this with the beat-boundary bridge: one opening sentence can do both jobs. A loop that is closed silently reads as a non sequitur.

**Visual continuity — keep the viewer anchored through explanations.**

When narration explains a mechanism, process, system, comparison, or causal chain, maintain the same visual anchor across adjacent explanation sentences. Use the same noun phrase for the anchor every time you name it, so the visual director can reuse it.

Examples:

- `[visual cue: fungal network diagram, tree A highlighted]`
- `[same diagram: nutrient-transfer arrow added]`
- `[same diagram: tree B highlighted]`

For comparisons, use one persistent split-screen or side-by-side visual. For sequences, use one persistent numbered list or timeline.

Multiple narration sentences may share one visual cue when no visual update is needed. Do not apply continuity requirements to atmospheric or scene-setting narration.

## Language rules (spoken English for a global audience)

- Write clear, natural spoken English at approximately CEFR B1 to lower B2.
- Prefer common everyday words. Do not replace simple words with sophisticated synonyms.
- Keep most sentences between 8 and 15 words. Keep every sentence below 25 words.
- Put one main idea in each sentence. Prefer active voice and concrete verbs.
- Avoid idioms, culturally specific expressions, slang, literary phrasing, academic phrasing, dense noun phrases, and deeply nested clauses.
- Explain every unavoidable technical term immediately in simple language. Optimize for listening comprehension, not literary written style.
- Write numbers the way a voice would say them ("about twenty-five feet"). Years such as 1919 are fine, but only after the hook. Avoid symbols and abbreviations a voice engine may misread.
- Simplify language, not subject matter. Preserve accurate facts, useful detail, and the interesting parts of the topic.
- End narration with an intentional narrative ending/payoff. Do not put CTA in narration.
- Do NOT include a callToAction field in your output. The CTA is configuration-owned and the pipeline appends it. Never write or invent CTA text.

EVIDENCE LANGUAGE RULES (critical):
Match the phrasing of every claim to its classification. Uncertainty is a storytelling asset — describe it honestly and engagingly, never flatten it into fake certainty.

- verified → state it plainly: "The island was abandoned in 1953."
- likely → use "evidence suggests", "most researchers believe", "strong evidence points to".
- debated → name the disagreement: "Historians still disagree about why", "Two competing explanations remain".
- popular_theory → attribute it as a theory: "One popular theory claims X — but no evidence has ever confirmed it."
- unverified_claim → say it is unconfirmed: "According to an unverified report", "No one has confirmed whether".
- myth → flag the myth and the truth: "It's widely believed that X — in fact, no. ..."
- false → only mention if the video debunks it: "The idea that X is simply not true."

Forbidden: "Nobody knows why this happened", "Scientists think...", "This was built by aliens", "It's a mystery" used as a dead end. Rewrite as above — specific, attributed, and honest.

BEAT PRIORITY RULES:

- HIGH priority beats are the most important moments. Give them the most narration emphasis, more words, and dramatic pacing. The audience must remember these.
- MEDIUM priority beats support the narrative. Give them normal treatment.
- LOW priority beats are supplementary. Compress or combine them with adjacent beats if needed to fit the time limit.
